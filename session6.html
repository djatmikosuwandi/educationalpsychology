02
Evolution of Learning Science

From Observable Behavior to Internal Mental Processes

During the early 20th century, psychology was dominated by Behaviorism (represented by Watson, Skinner, and Thorndike). Behaviorists focused exclusively on outward, directly measurable actions. Learning was defined strictly as an observable change in behavior resulting from environmental stimulus-response associations (S → R) and external reinforcers. The internal workings of the mind—such as attention, thoughts, mental images, and reasoning—were intentionally set aside because scientists lacked the tools to observe them directly.

The Cognitive Revolution of the 1950s and 1960s (championed by Jean Piaget, Jerome Bruner, Noam Chomsky, and George Miller) established that treating learners as passive responders ignored what is most essential: how the mind actually structures knowledge. Learners are active information processors who attend to, interpret, synthesize, and store knowledge into rich mental schemas.

03
Atkinson-Shiffrin & Baddeley

Human Cognitive Architecture

The Information Processing Model views human cognition as a coordinated tri-partite system: sensory buffers, limited working memory, and vast long-term memory, governed by metacognitive control.

Phase 1

Sensory Register

Duration: 0.5s (Iconic/Visual) to 3s (Echoic/Auditory).

Capacity: Extremely large, but traces vanish instantly without conscious focus.

Gateway: Selective Attention filters out 99% of environmental noise.

EdTech Tip: Use visual cueing (arrows, high contrast) in video tutorials to capture instantaneous attention.
Critical Bottleneck
Phase 2

Working Memory (WM)

Duration: 15 – 30 seconds without active rehearsal.

Capacity: Miller ($7 \pm 2$) or Cowan's modern benchmark of $4 \pm 1$ novel units.

Baddeley's Model: Phonological Loop, Visuospatial Sketchpad, Central Executive, Episodic Buffer.

EdTech Rule: Avoid split-attention. Never force learners to read dense subtitles while watching animations.
Phase 3

Long-Term Memory (LTM)

Duration: Lifetime storage.

Capacity: Virtually infinite.

Structure: Organized into hierarchical Schemas. Schemas compress thousands of facts into a single manageable unit.

EdTech Tip: Use retrieval practice (quizzes, reflective pause points) to reinforce neural schema retrieval paths.

Sweller (1988) & Mayer (2020)

Cognitive Load Theory & Multimedia Principles

John Sweller established that because working memory has hard structural limits, instructional design must manage how mental capacity is occupied. Total cognitive load is defined by the formula:

CLTotal = CLIntrinsic + CLExtraneous + CLGermane ≤ CapacityWM
Intrinsic Load (IL)

Origin: Inherent difficulty of the subject matter and element interactivity.

Instructional Strategy: Manage through segmenting (pacing) and sequencing simple-to-complex.

Extraneous Load (EL)

Origin: Poor interface layouts, decorative visuals, redundant text, and chaotic navigation.

Instructional Strategy: Aggressively eliminate. Every ounce of EL steals space from schema building.

Germane Load (GL)

Origin: Productive mental work dedicated to processing and integrating new knowledge into schemas.

Instructional Strategy: Foster through worked examples, self-explanation prompts, and analogy mapping.

Piaget & Ausubel

Schema Theory & Meaningful Learning

Jean Piaget: Cognitive Adaptation Cycle

Learning occurs when students cycle through mental balance and productive cognitive conflict:

1. Assimilation: Incorporating new observations into existing schemas without altering the baseline structure.
2. Cognitive Disequilibrium: When new information conflicts with existing beliefs, creating curiosity and cognitive tension.
3. Accommodation: Restructuring the schema or engineering a new one to fit novel data.
4. Equilibration: Reaching a higher, more sophisticated level of cognitive stability.

David Ausubel: Meaningful Learning

The most important single factor influencing learning is what the student already knows (prior knowledge).

Rote Memorization: Facts are memorized verbatim without linking to pre-existing anchors. Result: rapid decay and zero transfer ability.
Meaningful Learning (Subsumption): New facts bind deliberately to existing cognitive nodes (subsumers), enabling lifelong retention and creative problem-solving.
Advance Organizers: High-level introductory frameworks presented before new material to act as mental bridges between what learners know and what they must grasp.

Flavell (1979) & Zimmerman (2002)

Metacognition & Self-Regulated Learning (SRL)

Metacognition is commonly defined as "thinking about one's own thinking"—the conscious monitoring and regulation of one's own cognitive processes. In digital online learning, where external physical supervision is reduced, student mastery hinges upon the Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) Cycle:

1

Forethought & Planning

  • Goal setting & milestone mapping.
  • Strategy selection (e.g., self-quizzing vs skimming).
  • Self-efficacy & motivation assessment.
2

Performance Control

  • Attention focusing & digital distraction shielding.
  • Self-instruction and step-by-step checkpointing.
  • Active elaborative note-taking.
3

Self-Reflection

  • Self-evaluation against rubric standards.
  • Attribution of errors to effort vs strategy.
  • Adapting study habits for subsequent modules.

Theory Into Practice

Educational Technology Integration Matrix

Filter design principles:
Cognitive Principle Cognitive Phenomenon Common EdTech Flaw Instructional Solution
Dual Channels (Baddeley) Processing bottlenecks across auditory and visual channels. Instructor reading dense on-screen slide text aloud during webinars. Use minimal visual diagrams on slides while providing spoken narration.
Sweller's CLT Extraneous cognitive overload from interface noise. LMS screens with multiple sidebars, animated ads, and nested menus. Apply progressive disclosure; reveal advanced settings only on demand.
Ausubel's Subsumption Absence of anchoring mental nodes for abstract facts. Teaching programming syntax or math formulas without real-world context. Open lessons with visual advance organizers and everyday analogies.
Piaget's Disequilibrium Passive compliance with zero intrinsic curiosity. Delivering long, monotonic lecture videos with no pause questions. Start with paradoxes, counter-intuitive data sets, or anomaly puzzles.
Flavell's Metacognition Illusion of competence (feeling knowledgeable by simply watching). Students speed-watching videos without testing their comprehension. Embed mandatory interactive check-in questions directly in the timeline.

Student Audit Worksheet Tasks

Question 1 • Cognitive Load Analysis

Identify 3 specific sources of Extraneous Load in the default interface and explain the impact on the visuospatial sketchpad.

Model Solution / Rubric Standard:
  • Three Sources: (1) Continuous EDM background music violates Coherence Principle; (2) Scrolling bottom ticker competes for visual saccades; (3) Animated stickers in social chat distract selective attention.
  • Visuospatial impact: When reading the scrolling ticker while tracking vector animation, the visuospatial sketchpad experiences bottleneck collision, causing cognitive overload.
Question 2 • Instructional Redesign

Propose 2 interface redesigns based on Mayer's Principles and apply Visual Cueing (Signalling).

Model Solution / Rubric Standard:
  • Redesign 1 (Modality Principle): Remove the scrolling text ticker; explain vectors using clear verbal narration synchronized to the motion.
  • Redesign 2 (Coherence Principle): Mute the EDM background music completely and collapse animated chat during instruction.
  • Signalling: Highlight the active force vector in bold yellow when the narrator discusses mass and acceleration.
Question 3 • Advance Organizer Construction

Draft a short introductory analogy (Advance Organizer) explaining Newton's First Law (Inertia) to facilitate Meaningful Learning.

Exemplar Advance Organizer:

"Think of riding a city bus standing in the aisle. When the bus driver suddenly slams on the brakes, your feet stop with the floor, but your upper body lurches forward. Your body didn't get pushed by an invisible hand; it simply wanted to keep doing what it was already doing—moving forward. That resistance to changing state is what physicists call Inertia."
